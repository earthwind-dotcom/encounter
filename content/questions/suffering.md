If you're asking this because something terrible has happened to you or someone you love,
read this part first: you don't need an argument right now. You need people, and time, and
maybe permission to be angry. Everything below can wait. If you'd like to talk to a real
person, [you can](/talk).

If you're asking it as a question you can't get past, that's fair. It's the oldest and most
serious objection there is.

## The Bible asks it too

People are often surprised to find that the Bible doesn't dodge this. Whole books are
arguments with God about it.

**Job** loses everything, and his friends explain why: he must have done something wrong.
The book says they're wrong. Job accuses God to his face. At the end, God shows up and
rebukes the friends with the tidy answers, not Job.

**Psalm 88** is a prayer that ends in the dark. No turnaround, no "but I will praise you."
Its last word is darkness. It's in the Bible anyway, as a prayer you're allowed to pray.

**Jesus himself** wept at a friend's grave (John 11:35), even though the story says he was
about to raise him. And on the cross he quoted a psalm of abandonment: "My God, my God, why
have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34, quoting Psalm 22).

The Bible gives you permission to protest. It treats lament as a form of faith, not the
absence of it.

## What thinkers have said

There are some real ideas worth knowing about, held loosely:

- **Freedom.** A world with real love needs real freedom, and real freedom means people can
  do real harm. This goes some way toward explaining evil people do. It doesn't explain
  cancer or earthquakes.
- **A world that's in process.** A world with stable physical laws, where tectonic plates
  make continents and cells divide, is also a world where those same processes can hurt us.
- **We're not in a position to see the whole.** True, but cold comfort at a funeral.

Most philosophers now think the strict version of the argument, that God and evil logically
can't coexist, doesn't work. But the weight of it, the sheer amount of suffering, is still a
real challenge. Anyone who says it isn't is not being honest with you.

## What Christianity actually offers

Not an explanation for every tragedy. Something else.

A God who isn't watching from a distance. In Jesus, Christians believe God entered the world
at its worst: poverty, occupation, betrayal, torture, death. The cross means that whatever
you're going through, God isn't a stranger to it.

And a promise about the ending. The Bible's last picture isn't of people escaping the world,
but of God living with people in a world put right, where "he will wipe every tear from their
eyes, and death will be no more" (Revelation 21:4). Not an explanation of the pain. An end
to it.

## Where this leaves you

Suffering drives some people away from God and others toward him. Both are honest responses.
If you're somewhere in the middle, you're in good company, including many people in the Bible.

Try this: say one true sentence to God, out loud, even if it's "I don't know if you're there,
and if you are, I'm angry." That's a real prayer. The Psalms are full of them.
