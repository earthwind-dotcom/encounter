If you've read Joshua, or the end of Psalm 137, you know. There are passages in the Bible where
God seems to command the killing of whole populations, children included. If that doesn't
bother you, something's wrong.

It bothered Christians long before it bothered you. Here are the honest options.

## Read them as what they are

These are ancient war stories. Across the ancient Near East, kings wrote victory reports in
sweeping language: "we utterly destroyed them," "not one survived." Scholars who compare them
find it was a convention, like a sports fan saying their team "annihilated" the other one.

The Bible shows signs of the same thing. Joshua says the land was completely conquered. The
very next book, Judges, opens by listing all the peoples who were still there (Judges 1). And
archaeology doesn't show a single sweeping conquest. That doesn't make the language less
troubling, but it does mean you may be reading it more literally than its first audience did.

## Notice where the Bible argues back

The Old Testament isn't one voice. It's a conversation, and sometimes an argument.

- Deuteronomy says no Moabite may enter the assembly (Deuteronomy 23:3). The book of **Ruth**
  makes a Moabite woman the great-grandmother of King David.
- **Jonah** is a story about a prophet furious that God showed mercy to Israel's most brutal
  enemy, Nineveh. The book ends with God asking Jonah: shouldn't I care about that great city?
- The prophets repeatedly condemn violence done in God's name.

The Bible doesn't hide its hardest texts. It also doesn't let them have the last word.

## Look at God through Jesus

Christians believe Jesus is the clearest picture of God there is. "Whoever has seen me has
seen the Father" (John 14:9). When his disciples wanted to call down fire on a village that
rejected them, Jesus turned and rebuked them (Luke 9:54–55). He told people to love their
enemies. He died forgiving the people killing him.

Christians disagree about exactly how to hold this together. Some defend the conquest texts as
justice in a brutal world. Others see them as Israel's partial, human understanding of God,
which Jesus corrects. What they share is this: the full picture of God isn't found in Joshua.
It's found in Jesus.

## Where this leaves you

You don't have to make peace with every page before you take Jesus seriously. You're allowed
to read these texts, be troubled by them, and bring that to God directly. People in the Bible
did exactly that. Start with Jesus, then read the rest in his light.
