You've probably heard it both ways. One friend says Jesus plainly claimed to be God, so he was
either lying, crazy, or telling the truth. Another says Jesus never claimed any such thing; the
church made him divine centuries later, at a council, by a vote.

Neither is quite right. The real picture is more interesting.

## What he didn't say

In the three earliest Gospels (Mark, Matthew and Luke), Jesus never says "I am God." He talks
far more about God's kingdom than about himself. He often tells people not to spread word
about what he's done. If you're looking for a single, explicit proof-text in Mark, you won't
find it.

John's Gospel is different. There Jesus says "Before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58) and "The
Father and I are one" (10:30), and Thomas calls him "my Lord and my God" (20:28). John was
written later, and scholars debate how much of it reflects Jesus' own words and how much the
author's deep reflection on them.

## What he did

Here's what people miss. In the earliest Gospels, Jesus keeps doing things that, in his world,
belonged to God alone.

- **He forgave sins on his own authority.** When he tells a paralysed man "your sins are
  forgiven," the religious teachers react instantly: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?"
  (Mark 2:7). They understood exactly what he was doing.
- **He spoke over the law.** "You have heard it said... but I tell you." Prophets said "thus
  says the Lord." Jesus said "I tell you."
- **He claimed Daniel's heavenly figure.** At his trial, asked if he is the Messiah, he answers
  with Daniel 7: the "Son of Man" coming with the clouds of heaven, sharing God's throne (Mark
  14:62). The high priest calls it blasphemy.
- **He called himself "lord of the Sabbath"** (Mark 2:28), the day God had made holy.

None of these is a sentence that says "I am God." All of them are acts that only make sense if
he believed he stood in God's place.

## The puzzle of the first Christians

The earliest Christians were Jews who recited every day that there is one God. They would have
died rather than worship a man. And yet, within about twenty years of the crucifixion, they
were praying to Jesus, singing hymns to him, and calling him "Lord," the word their Greek
Bible used for God's own name.

Paul, writing in the 50s, takes the central confession of Judaism, "the Lord our God, the Lord
is one," and puts Jesus inside it: "one God, the Father... and one Lord, Jesus Christ" (1
Corinthians 8:6). He quotes what looks like an even earlier hymn saying Jesus "was in the form
of God" and that every knee will bow to him (Philippians 2:6–11), language Isaiah used for God
alone.

Larry Hurtado spent a career showing how early and how Jewish this devotion was. The church
didn't slowly promote Jesus to divinity over centuries. Something made his first followers
worship him almost at once.

## The honest debate

Bart Ehrman argues that Jesus saw himself as a prophet and a coming king, not as God, and that
his followers' belief in the resurrection led them to exalt him. Scholars like Hurtado and
Bauckham argue that the speed and shape of early devotion point back to Jesus himself. It's a
real debate between serious people, and you should read both sides.

What both sides agree on: the question "who is he?" was live from the very beginning.

## Where this leaves you

C. S. Lewis famously said you can't call Jesus merely a good teacher: a man who said what he
said would be something else entirely. You don't need that argument to be airtight to feel its
weight. Read Mark 2 and Mark 14 for yourself and notice what he claims by what he does. Then ask
the question his first followers couldn't avoid: who is this?
