The picture most people carry is roughly this: when you die, your soul leaves your body and goes
to heaven, which is somewhere up there, with clouds and harps. Your body stays behind and
doesn't matter anymore.

It's a comforting picture for some and a boring one for others. It's also closer to Plato than
to the Bible.

## The big hope is resurrection

When the New Testament talks about the future, its main word isn't "heaven." It's
"resurrection." Paul spends a whole chapter on it (1 Corinthians 15): as Jesus was raised
bodily, so those who belong to him will be raised, with bodies made new and no longer subject to
death.

And it isn't only people. Paul says creation itself "groans" as it waits to be set free (Romans
8:18–25). The Bible's last scene isn't souls leaving the earth; it's a new heaven and a new earth,
with God living among people, every tear wiped away, and death gone (Revelation 21:1–4).

The direction of travel is God coming to put this world right, not people escaping it.

## You're not a soul trapped in a body

The Hebrew word often translated "soul," *nephesh*, mostly means a living being: the whole person,
breathing and alive. In Genesis 2:7, God breathes into dust and the man becomes a living
*nephesh*. You don't *have* a nephesh; you *are* one. That's why bodily resurrection matters so
much in the Bible. A person without a body isn't a whole person.

## What about in between?

Here the Bible says less than you might expect. Jesus tells the man crucified next to him,
"today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). Paul says that to die is to "be with
Christ, which is far better" (Philippians 1:23), and he also describes the dead as "asleep"
(1 Thessalonians 4:13). Christians have understood this in-between state in different ways:
conscious rest with Christ, or something like sleep until the resurrection. Both are held by
serious people. What they share is that it's temporary and safe, and that the story isn't over.

## And the rapture?

Many people grew up with the idea that believers will suddenly vanish, taken up before a time of
terrible tribulation. That specific teaching was developed in the 19th century and spread widely
through study Bibles and later through novels and films. Most Christians through history, and
most today, don't read the Bible that way. The passage it's built on (1 Thessalonians 4:17) most
likely pictures people going out to meet a returning king and escort him back, not leaving.

## Where this leaves you

If you've been told that Christianity is about getting your soul out of here, the real hope is
bigger and more physical: this world, and you in it, made whole. It's grounded in one claim, that
Jesus was raised first. If that's true, death isn't the last word for anyone who belongs to him.
If you want to look at the evidence for that claim, [start here](/questions/resurrection).
