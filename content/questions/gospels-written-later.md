This is one of the first things people learn in an intro religion class, and it can land like
a trapdoor. You grew up thinking Matthew, Mark, Luke and John were four friends of Jesus
taking notes. Then someone tells you the books were anonymous, written decades later, and the
floor goes.

Here's the honest picture. Some of it will unsettle you. Some of it might surprise you the
other way.

## What's true

**The dates.** Most scholars date Mark to around 70, Matthew and Luke to the 80s, and John
to the 90s. Jesus died around 30. So you're looking at a gap of roughly forty to seventy
years.

**The names.** None of the four Gospels names its author in the text. The titles we know
("According to Mark," and so on) are firmly attested by around 180, when Irenaeus, a bishop
in what's now France, lists all four. Whether those names reflect real memory of who was
behind each book is a genuine argument among serious scholars.

**They're shaped.** Each Gospel writer arranged and framed his material to make a point. John
says so outright: "these are written so that you may come to believe" (John 20:31). Luke
says he researched and wrote an "orderly account" (Luke 1:1–4). Nobody was writing neutral
news.

## What people often leave out

**Forty years is living memory.** Think about something that happened forty years ago in
your family. People who were there are still telling the story. Paul, writing in the 50s,
says he personally met Peter and James (Galatians 1:18–19), and that most of a group of five
hundred people who said they'd seen the risen Jesus were still alive (1 Corinthians 15:6). In
other words: go and ask them.

**It's early by ancient standards.** Our main surviving biographies of Alexander the Great
were written by Plutarch and Arrian, roughly four hundred years after he died. Historians
still use them. A forty-year gap is close.

**There's older material underneath.** In 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, Paul quotes a summary he says
he "received": Christ died, was buried, was raised, and appeared to Peter, then the Twelve,
then others. Most scholars think that formula was fixed within a few years of the events.
Matthew and Luke also used earlier written sources, including Mark.

**"Shaped" isn't "made up."** Every history ever written is shaped for a purpose. The Gospels
look like ancient biographies, the kind of writing that tried to capture who someone really
was, often by arranging sayings and events for effect. That's a different thing from fiction.

## Where the real argument is

The live debate isn't "are the Gospels reliable, yes or no." It's how memory works. One side,
represented well by Richard Bauckham, argues the Gospels stay close to named eyewitnesses. The
other, represented well by Bart Ehrman, argues that memories shift every time a story is
retold, so the Gospels preserve the gist more than the details. Both are worth reading. Both
agree you're looking at memories of a real person, told by people who thought he was
extraordinary.

## Where this leaves you

You don't have to pretend the Gospels are something they're not. You can read them as what
they are: four portraits of one man, from people who knew people who knew him, written to
convince you he matters. The only way to find out if they're convincing is to read one. Mark
is the shortest and the earliest. Try reading it in one sitting and notice what you notice.
