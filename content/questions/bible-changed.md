The idea goes like this: the Bible has been copied by hand, translated, retranslated and edited
by the church for two thousand years. It's a game of telephone. Whatever it originally said is
long gone.

It's a reasonable worry. Here's what the evidence actually shows.

## It's not a translation of a translation

Your English or Spanish Bible wasn't translated from Latin, which was translated from Greek,
which was translated from Aramaic. Modern translators work directly from the oldest Hebrew,
Aramaic and Greek manuscripts available, and they tell you in the footnotes when the manuscripts
disagree. The telephone picture doesn't describe how it works.

## There are a lot of manuscripts

For most ancient books, historians have a handful of copies, often made many centuries after the
original. For the New Testament, more than five thousand Greek manuscripts survive, whole or in
fragments, plus early translations into Latin, Syriac and Coptic, and thousands of quotations in
early Christian writers. Some fragments date to within a century or so of the originals.

That abundance cuts both ways, and it's worth being honest about it.

## Yes, there are many differences

Because there are so many copies, there are many differences between them: by some counts more
differences than there are words in the New Testament. That sounds alarming. Here's what they
mostly are:

- spelling (like "color" and "colour")
- word order (in Greek, it often doesn't change the meaning)
- a word skipped or repeated by a tired copyist
- a copyist "correcting" a passage to match another Gospel

A small number do matter, and scholars know exactly which ones. The biggest are two whole
passages that weren't in the earliest manuscripts: the longer ending of Mark (16:9–20), and the
story of the woman caught in adultery (John 7:53–8:11). Open a modern Bible and you'll find
both marked, usually in brackets with a note.

That's the opposite of a cover-up. It's the evidence laid out in plain sight.

## How scholars work back to the original

Because the manuscripts come from different regions and centuries, scholars can compare them,
see where a change entered, and work back to the earliest form of the text. This field is
called textual criticism, and it's practiced by believers and skeptics alike, using the same
methods. Bart Ehrman, a skeptic, co-wrote the standard textbook with Bruce Metzger, a believer.

## The Old Testament too

Before 1947, the oldest complete Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament dated to around the
year 1000. Then the Dead Sea Scrolls were found, including a complete scroll of Isaiah roughly
a thousand years older. Compared side by side, the two are remarkably close. A thousand years of
copying, and the text had held.

## Where this leaves you

The Bible has a history, and it's a human one: copyists, slips, additions, and centuries of
careful work to recover what was first written. You don't have to pretend otherwise. What that
history shows is a text we can know with remarkable confidence, with the uncertain places
openly marked. The real question isn't whether the words survived. It's what you'll make of the
person they're about.
