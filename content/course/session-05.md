**The question**
If you had to sum up what Jesus was on about, in one sentence, what would it be?

**The text: Mark 1:14–15**
> After John was arrested, Jesus came into Galilee announcing God's good news: "The time is
> up. The kingdom of God has come near. Turn around, and trust the good news."
> *(a close rendering; read it in any modern translation)*

*Kind of writing: a headline. Mark gives Jesus' whole message in two sentences, before a single story. Everything after is meant to be read in its light.*

**Then read side by side:** Matthew 5:3–10 and Luke 6:20–26 (the Beatitudes, in two versions).

**What we know**
- "Kingdom of God" was Jesus' main theme. It appears far more often in his sayings than any other phrase. *[consensus]*
- It doesn't mean a place you go when you die. It means God's rule: God putting the world right, and doing it here. The prayer he taught says "your kingdom come... on earth as in heaven." *[consensus]*
- To people living under Roman occupation, "kingdom" was political language. There was already a kingdom running their lives. *[consensus]*
- Jesus talked about the kingdom as both arriving now (in what he did) and still to come in full. Scholars call this "already and not yet." *[strong majority]*
- Luke has "Blessed are you who are poor." Matthew has "Blessed are the poor in spirit." Both Gospel writers shaped a shared tradition for their own readers. Which wording is closer to Jesus' own is debated. *[debated]*

**Try this**
The examen, an old practice from the 1500s. Tonight, walk back through your day slowly. Name
one moment you're grateful for. Name one you'd do differently. That's it.

**For the table**
1. What does Jesus actually announce in Mark 1:15? What does he ask people to do about it?
2. If you'd lived under an occupying army, how would "the kingdom of God has come near" have sounded?
3. Read the two Beatitude lists again. Which one lands harder for you, and why?
4. Someone says: "Jesus was a spiritual teacher; the political stuff got added later." What would you say?

**One next step**
The examen, each night this week. Two minutes is enough.

<!-- facilitator -->

**Driving question.** Most people in the room think Christianity is about going to heaven when
you die, and that Jesus' message was "believe in me so you can get there." This session is a
quiet shock: Jesus' own headline was about God's rule arriving on earth. That reframes
everything that follows in Unit B, and it prepares the ground for session 10, where "what
his death means" will only make sense inside the kingdom story.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 15 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 2 · Close 3.

**The text, how to read it.** Read Mark 1:14–15 aloud twice. Point out three things before
anyone interprets: the timing ("after John was arrested", so the announcement starts in a
dangerous moment), the claim ("the time is up", "has come near"), and the demand ("turn
around", "trust"). Then hand out the Beatitudes side by side and let people spend two quiet
minutes circling differences. They will find them: Luke is second person ("you who are
poor"), Matthew third person; Luke has woes to match the blessings; Matthew has "in spirit"
and "hunger and thirst for righteousness."

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *"Kingdom" is the centre.* Jesus' most repeated theme is the kingdom (or reign) of God.
   Historians across the range agree on this, and it's the backbone of E. P. Sanders's and
   N. T. Wright's portraits alike, very different scholars. *[consensus]*

2. *It's about God's rule, not a destination.* The phrase is dynamic: God reigning, acting,
   setting things right. The Lord's Prayer asks for it to come "on earth as in heaven."
   Matthew's "kingdom of heaven" is the same thing in a more reverent Jewish idiom that avoids
   saying God's name directly; it's not a different place. *[consensus]*

3. *It was political, and hopeful.* Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas under Rome, with taxes,
   debt and soldiers. The Hebrew prophets had promised a day when God would reign and put
   things right (Isaiah 52:7 is a good one to read: "your God reigns"). Announcing that this
   reign "has come near" was good news to the poor and a threat to the powerful. That tension
   is part of why he was killed (session 8). *[consensus on the setting]*

4. *Already and not yet.* Jesus speaks of the kingdom as arriving in his healings and meals
   ("if I cast out demons by the finger of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you,"
   Luke 11:20) and also as still to come (the prayer, "your kingdom come"). Most scholars
   accept both strands go back to Jesus; what he expected about timing is genuinely debated,
   and you can say so. *[strong majority on both strands; debated on timing]*

5. *Two Beatitude lists, one tradition.* Matthew and Luke each draw on a shared source and
   shape it. Luke's blunt "you who are poor" and his woes fit his concern for the economically
   poor throughout his Gospel. Matthew's "poor in spirit" and "hunger for righteousness" fit
   his concern with the kind of person a disciple becomes. Whether Jesus said one or both, or
   something behind both, is debated. What's worth drawing out: the Gospels show their shaping
   openly, and it doesn't erase the edge. Both lists turn the world's rankings upside down.
   *[consensus that both edited a shared tradition; debated which is closer]*

6. *One word on "meek".* Matthew 5:5 quotes Psalm 37:11, and *praus* doesn't mean weak. Point
   to the Roots entry on the site if people want more.

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "This one's nearly five hundred years old. Ignatius of
Loyola taught it to people who were busy, not monks. It isn't about grading your day. It's
about noticing. If the kingdom is arriving in ordinary places, the examen is a way to learn to
spot it." Walk them through it once in the room, with a minute of silence for each half.

**Likely objections.**
- *"So Jesus was a political revolutionary?"* Not in the sense of armed revolt; he refused
  that (and session 6 is about exactly this). But his message had public consequences, and the
  Romans treated him as a threat. "Spiritual versus political" is a modern split he wouldn't
  have recognised.
- *"If Matthew changed Luke's words, how can I trust either?"* Careful: neither copied the
  other here; both shaped a common source. Ancient biographers were expected to arrange and
  frame. And the substance survives the shaping: blessing on the poor, reversal of status.
- *"Didn't Jesus expect the world to end right away, and get it wrong?"* A serious scholarly
  position (Albert Schweitzer made it famous, and Dale Allison has argued a version of it). It
  is genuinely debated. Don't dodge; label it, and note that the "already" strand is also
  early and strong.
- *"Isn't 'kingdom' just a nice way of saying 'heaven'?"* Read the Lord's Prayer line again.
  The direction is heaven coming to earth.

**Facilitation notes.**
- This session can feel like a relief to people burned by "turn or burn" preaching, and like a
  threat to people who were taught that the only point is getting into heaven. Name both gently.
- Don't let it become a politics night. The point is that Jesus' message was public and good
  news for the poor, not which party that favours today.
- Light invitation only: "What he announced is still on offer. You can talk to any of us about
  it any time."
