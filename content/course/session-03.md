**The question**
Strip the church away. What can a historian actually say?

**The text: Mark 1:9–11**
> In those days Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee and was baptized by John in the Jordan.
> And just as he was coming up out of the water, he saw the heavens torn apart and the
> Spirit descending like a dove on him. And a voice came from heaven, "You are my Son, the
> Beloved; with you I am well pleased." *(NRSV)*

*Kind of writing: the opening of a Gospel. Mark has no birth story and no genealogy. He starts with a grown man walking out of an unremarkable village.*

**Two outsiders mention him**
- **Tacitus**, a Roman senator and historian, writing around 116 CE about Nero blaming Christians for the fire of Rome in 64: "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus." He is hostile. He calls the movement "a most mischievous superstition." *[consensus that the passage is authentic]*
- **Josephus**, a Jewish historian writing in the 90s, mentions Jesus twice. The longer passage as we have it contains obvious Christian additions. The shorter one, referring to "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James," is the more secure of the two. *[consensus that Josephus wrote something; debated exactly what]*

**What nearly every historian grants** *[consensus]*
Jesus was a Galilean Jew. He was baptized by John. He taught in parables and short sayings.
He gathered followers. He had a reputation in his own lifetime as a healer and exorcist,
whatever one concludes about that. He caused some kind of disturbance at the Temple. He was
crucified in Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate, around 30 CE. Soon afterward his followers were
claiming they had seen him alive.

That list is granted by scholars across the whole range, from devout Christians to
committed atheists.

**What is genuinely open** *[debated]*
What he thought he was. Whether he expected the world to end soon. What actually happened
at the trial. What to make of the miracle traditions. These are real arguments among
serious people, not a believer-versus-skeptic split.

**Try this**
Five minutes of silence this week. Not praying at anything in particular. Sit, set a timer,
and notice what your mind does with the quiet.

**For the table**
1. Does it change anything for you that a hostile Roman source mentions the execution?
2. The list above is shorter than the Gospels. Is a short, secure list more useful to you than a long, contested one?
3. Which item on the "genuinely open" list would you most want answered?

Two more five-minute sittings before we meet again.

**Driving question.** Session 1 asked whether the documents can be trusted. This one asks
what survives if you interrogate them the way you would any ancient source. People arrive
assuming the answer is "almost nothing." The answer is actually "a surprising amount, and
less than the creed." Both halves matter.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 15 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 2 · Close 3.

**The text, how to read it.** Read Mark 1:9–11 aloud, then say what is strange about it.
Mark opens his book by having Jesus baptized by somebody else, in a baptism the other
Gospels describe as being about repentance. That is an awkward way to start a book arguing
this man is God's Son.

Then show them the discomfort spreading through the tradition. Matthew keeps the baptism
but adds John protesting that he should be baptized by Jesus instead. Luke mentions it
almost in passing, in a subordinate clause, after John has already been imprisoned. John's
Gospel never narrates the baptism at all; the Baptist only testifies about what he saw.

That progression is visible on the page, and it is the single most persuasive thing you can
show a skeptical newcomer in fifteen minutes. Nobody invents a detail they then spend
decades softening.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *The outside sources are real but thin.* Tacitus confirms a founder executed under
   Pilate in Tiberius's reign. Josephus mentions Jesus twice; the longer passage has been
   worked over by Christian copyists, and the honest position is that Josephus wrote
   something and we cannot fully recover it. The shorter reference to James is more secure.
   Do not oversell these. They establish that Jesus existed and was executed, which is
   worth having, and little more. *[consensus]*

2. *The real evidence is the Gospels, read critically.* Historians do not throw out a
   source because it has a viewpoint; every ancient source has one. They ask what it is
   likely to have got right. *[consensus]*

3. *How historians have argued.* For most of the last century the tools were "criteria":
   material attested in independent sources, material embarrassing to the people
   transmitting it, material that fits a first-century Galilean setting. The baptism and
   the crucifixion are the textbook cases of embarrassment. Both are things the early
   church had to explain rather than things it would invent. *[the criteria were standard;
   see the caution below]*

4. *An honest caution about the method.* Over the last fifteen years the criteria approach
   has taken heavy criticism from within the field, on the grounds that it treats tradition
   as separable into authentic and inauthentic fragments in a way memory does not work. The
   newer direction argues from broad recurrent patterns instead: what shows up again and
   again across the material. It is worth saying this out loud, because a group that is
   told "historians have a method" and later discovers the method is contested will feel
   handled. *[debated, and actively so]*

5. *The short list.* Walk the handout list. Then say plainly: notice what is on it and what
   is not. The resurrection is not on it. What is on it is that his followers, very soon
   after, were claiming to have seen him. What to make of that claim is session 9, and we
   are not going to pretend history settles it tonight.

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "We have been using our heads hard for three weeks.
This one uses nothing. Five minutes, a timer, no words, no goal. You are not trying to
achieve a state. You are finding out what is in there when you stop adding to it." Warn
them it is more uncomfortable than it sounds, and that being bad at it is not failing it.

**Likely objections.**
- *"Didn't he maybe not exist at all?"* This is not a live position in the field. Ehrman, who is not a Christian, wrote a book against it. The crucifixion and the brother named James are the hard anchors. Say it once, calmly, and move on; do not spend the table on it.
- *"Josephus is a forgery."* Partly, in the longer passage, and that is openly acknowledged by everyone. The overreach is going from "edited" to "entirely fabricated." Note that the James passage is separately attested and much less disputed.
- *"If historians can't prove the resurrection, why bother with history?"* Because it clears the ground. History can establish that a real man was really executed and that people who knew him made an extraordinary claim within a very short time. It cannot adjudicate the claim. Knowing which questions the tool answers is not a weakness.
- *"So the Gospels are unreliable."* Careful here. "Shaped for a purpose" is not "unreliable." Every history ever written is shaped for a purpose.

**Facilitation notes.**
- Two opposite people are in the room. One wants the historical case to be airtight and will be unsettled that it is not. One expects it to be empty and will be unsettled that it is not. Speak to both.
- Resist apologetics reflexes. The measured version is more convincing than the maximal one, and the group can tell the difference.
- If someone asks what you personally think, answer, and label it as **my read**.
- The silence practice will produce the week's most honest table conversation next session. Ask about it then.
