**The question**
Do I have to have it all worked out before I can follow Jesus?

**The texts**
> "I believe. Help my unbelief!" *(Mark 9:24)*
>
> "When they saw him, they worshiped him, but some doubted." *(Matthew 28:17)*
>
> Thomas said, "Unless I see the nail marks in his hands... I won't believe." *(John 20:25)*

*Kind of writing: three scenes from three Gospels. None of them edits the doubt out.*

**What we know**
- The Gospels record doubt among Jesus' closest followers, including after the resurrection. They could have left it out. They didn't. *[consensus]*
- The Greek word usually translated "faith," *pistis*, means trust, faithfulness and loyalty, not only believing facts. *[strong majority]*
- In Matthew 28:17, the doubters are given the same mission as everyone else in the next verses. *[consensus on the text]*
- Doubt and trust coexist throughout the Psalms, which Jesus prayed. *[consensus]*
- *My read:* certainty isn't the entry requirement. Trust is, and trust can be small.

**Try this**
Write out your live doubts, plainly and specifically. Not to solve them. To see them. Then
take last week's paper (the thing you carry) and read it alongside.

**For the table**
1. What does Jesus do with each doubter in these three scenes?
2. Why would the Gospel writers keep "but some doubted" in their story?
3. If faith means trust and loyalty more than certainty, what changes for you?
4. Someone says: "If you still have doubts, you're not really a believer." Is that true?

**One next step**
Bring one doubt to the table next week, if you want to.

<!-- facilitator -->

**Driving question.** After sessions 9 and 10, some people in the room are close to saying yes
and are stuck on one thing: "I'm not sure enough." Others have already said yes and are
terrified their doubts mean it wasn't real. This session frees both groups.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 15 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 3 · Close 2.

**The text, how to read it.** Read the three scenes in full: Mark 9:14–29, Matthew
28:16–20, John 20:24–29. For each, ask: who doubts, what exactly do they doubt, and what does
Jesus do? He heals the boy anyway. He commissions the doubters anyway. He shows up for Thomas
and offers exactly what Thomas asked for. Nobody is sent away for doubting.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *The texts keep the doubt.* Matthew's "but some doubted" (28:17) is a striking thing to
   write at the climax of your Gospel. The Greek word (*distazō*) means hesitating, being in
   two minds. It may include some of the Eleven. Scholars debate exactly who; the text doesn't
   smooth it. *[consensus that the text includes doubt among the disciples; debated who]*

2. *Pistis is trust and allegiance.* In the Greco-Roman world, *pistis* was the word for
   loyalty to a patron or a king, reliability, faithfulness in a relationship. Christian "faith"
   is closer to "I'm putting my weight on you and I'm with you" than "I'm certain these
   propositions are true." Matthew Bates has argued this at length; Teresa Morgan's scholarly
   work on *pistis* in the Roman world supports the broad point. *[strong majority]*

3. *Certainty is not the opposite of doubt; refusal is the opposite of trust.* Peter Enns
   argues that the need for certainty can actually block trust. The Psalms model the other way:
   "How long, Lord? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13), followed by "But I trust in your
   steadfast love." *[my read, with good company]*

4. *What doubt is and isn't.* Some doubt is intellectual: is it true? Some is emotional: does
   God care about me? Some is moral: can I trust a God who allows this? They need different
   responses. Name them. The course has addressed the first; the table can hold the others.
   *[my read]*

5. *People of deep faith have doubted.* Mother Teresa's letters, published after her death,
   describe decades of feeling God's absence while she kept serving. Faith doesn't always feel
   like faith. *[consensus that the letters say this]*

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "Write your doubts down. Be specific: not 'I doubt
God', but 'I don't know how a good God allows my sister's illness.' You're not trying to fix
them tonight. You're getting them out where you can see them. Then read your paper from last
week alongside it. Notice: you can carry both."

**The invitation.** Brief and clear: "If you've been holding back from following Jesus because
you're not sure enough, hear this: you don't have to be sure. You have to be willing to trust
him with the questions. That's what the disciples did. The door's open."

**Likely objections.**
- *"Isn't this just lowering the bar?"* No. It's putting the bar where the New Testament puts
  it: trust and loyalty, which cost more than certainty, not less.
- *"If I still doubt after all this, maybe it's not for me."* Maybe. Or maybe you're in the
  same place as the people on that mountain. Don't decide for them.
- *"Doubt is a sin."* Not in these texts. Jesus treats it as something to meet, not punish.

**Facilitation notes.**
- If someone shares a serious doubt at the table, thank them. Don't rush to answer it.
- Some people's "doubt" is grief or anger wearing a philosophy costume. Listen for that.
- Follow up one to one with anyone who looks close to a decision.
