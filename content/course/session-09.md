**The question**
What exactly are we being asked to believe, and on what basis?

**The text — 1 Corinthians 15:3–8**
> For I handed on to you as of first importance what I in turn had received: that Christ
> died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures, and that he was buried, and that he
> was raised on the third day in accordance with the scriptures, and that he appeared to
> Cephas, then to the twelve. Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers and
> sisters at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have died. Then he
> appeared to James, then to all the apostles. Last of all, as to one untimely born, he
> appeared also to me. *(NRSV)*

*Kind of writing: not Paul's own words. He says he is passing on something he was given. This is a set formula, older than the letter it sits in.*

**The second text — Mark 16:8**
> So they went out and fled from the tomb, for terror and amazement had seized them; and
> they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid. *(NRSV)*

*Kind of writing: the end of Mark. The oldest copies stop here, mid-fear, with nobody telling anyone. The longer ending in your Bible is a later addition, and your footnotes will say so.*

**What we know**
- Jesus was executed by crucifixion under Pontius Pilate, around 30 CE. *[consensus]*
- The list Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15 is older than the letter. The language of receiving and handing on marks it as inherited material, and most scholars date it to within a few years of the execution. That is remarkably early for an ancient claim. *[strong majority]*
- Notice what that early list does not contain: no empty tomb, no women. The empty tomb comes to us through the Gospel accounts, which are later. The two strands of evidence are separate, and they are not equally strong. *[consensus that they are separate strands]*
- Something happened that convinced a scattered, frightened group that Jesus was alive, and convinced them fast enough and hard enough that they reorganised their lives around it and several of them died for it. That the disciples believed this is not seriously disputed. *[consensus]*
- What caused that belief is disputed, and this is where serious people separate. *[debated]*
- In the Jewish thought of that time, "resurrection" did not mean a ghost, and it did not mean "his memory lives on." It meant bodies, and it was expected at the end of history, for everyone at once. One person raised on their own, in the middle of time, is not what anyone was waiting for. *[strong majority]*

**Try this**
Write the letter to God you will not send. What would you actually ask, if you knew he was
reading it? Nobody else sees this.

**For the table**
1. Which is stronger evidence to you, the very early list Paul quotes, or the empty tomb accounts? Why?
2. If the disciples were sincerely mistaken, what do you think they experienced?
3. What would it take to move you, in either direction?

**One next step**
Keep the letter. Add to it during the week. If you want to talk to any of us this week
about any of it, we would be glad to.

**Driving question.** This is the hinge of the whole course. Everything before it has been
clearing ground. If you handle this session honestly, people will trust the rest. If you
oversell it, thoughtful people will quietly write off everything you said in the previous
eight weeks. The goal tonight is not to win. The goal is to lay the evidence out so fairly
that a person could walk either way, and then to say plainly what is being offered.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 25 · Invitation 10 · Practice 10 ·
Table 25 · Close 5.

**The text, how to read it.** Read 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 aloud first. Then say the thing
that makes it interesting: Paul is quoting. "I handed on to you what I in turn had
received" is technical language for passing along fixed tradition. So this list was already
circulating, already formulated, before Paul wrote it down in the mid 50s. Most scholars
put it within a few years of the crucifixion.

Then read Mark 16:1–8 and stop dead at verse 8. Let the room sit in it. The oldest Gospel
ends with an empty tomb, a young man in white, and three terrified women saying nothing to
anyone. No appearance. No reunion. Just fear.

Then have them look at their own Bible's footnote on 16:9–20. It will say the earliest
manuscripts lack it. Point out that this is printed in their Bible, not hidden from them.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *Two separate strands.* The appearances and the empty tomb are different pieces of
   evidence, from different sources, with different strength. The earliest thing we have,
   the list Paul quotes, is all appearances and no tomb. The tomb comes through the Gospel
   narratives. Keeping them separate is the single most useful habit you can teach tonight.
   *[consensus that they are distinct strands]*

2. *What is not in dispute.* That Jesus was crucified. That within a very short time his
   followers were saying publicly that he was alive, in Jerusalem, where the claim could be
   checked. That they believed it sincerely. Historians across the range grant this.
   *[consensus]*

3. *What "resurrection" meant then.* Not a spirit surviving. Not a comforting memory. Their
   word meant a body, and it was expected at the end of everything, for all God's people
   together. So the claim the disciples made was not the normal religious claim of their
   world. It was odd, and being odd is part of why it is interesting.
   *[strong majority]*

4. *Where the disagreement actually is.* Lay out the range and be fair to all of it.
   - Some historians argue that bodily resurrection is the best available explanation,
     because the shape of the early belief is so specific and so unlike what anyone
     expected. N. T. Wright argues this at length. *[one major view]*
   - Some argue the disciples had genuine visionary experiences, of a kind that grieving
     people do sometimes have, and that the movement grew from there. Gerd Lüdemann argues
     a version of this; Bart Ehrman argues that whatever happened, a historian cannot get
     behind the experiences to a miracle. *[one major view]*
   - Some sit genuinely in the middle. Dale Allison has spent decades on this and remains
     openly unresolved. He takes the evidence seriously, finds the tidy naturalistic
     explanations thin, and also finds the apologetic case overstated. He is worth naming
     out loud, because he shows a person can look at all of it carefully and not land.
     *[a real position, not a fudge]*
   - E. P. Sanders put it about as honestly as it can be put: that the followers had these
     experiences is a fact; what the reality was behind them, he did not claim to know.
     *[one major view]*

5. *What history can and cannot do.* A historian works with what is probable, and a
   one-time event that breaks the pattern is exactly what that method is worst at. That is
   not a dodge and it is not a trick. It means history can carry you to the edge: a real
   execution, a real and very early claim, sincere people. It cannot carry you across.
   Crossing is a decision made with the evidence, not one forced by it.
   *[consensus about the method's limits]*

**The invitation, and how to make it.** Do this after the brief and before the practice,
while the room is still holding the weight of it. Say something close to this, once:

> "If this happened, it is not neutral information you can file away. The claim is that
> Jesus is alive, and that he is asking you to trust him and follow him. That is what all
> of this has been heading toward, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.
>
> You do not have to do anything tonight. If you want to, it is not complicated: you tell
> him, in your own words, that you are in. If you are not there, that is a real answer, and
> you are genuinely welcome here for as long as you want to keep coming."

Then stop talking. Do not fill the silence. Do not soften it, repeat it, or add a second
ask. No heads bowed, no eyes closed, no hands, no spotlight. Move into the practice when
the silence has had its time. Anyone who wants to talk will find you afterward, and several
will.

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "Write the letter you will not send. Not a prayer,
not something you read out. If God were reading it, what would you actually ask him? Anger
counts. So does 'I do not know if you are there.' Keep it. We come back to it."

**Likely objections.**
- *"People see dead relatives all the time."* True, and it is an honest comparison worth taking seriously. Then note what does not fit it: those experiences normally comfort the grieving privately, they do not usually produce a public claim that the person has been bodily raised, and they do not usually reorganise a movement around it. Whether that difference is decisive is exactly the argument. Do not pretend it settles anything.
- *"They stole the body."* This was the earliest counter-explanation and it appears in Matthew, which tells you the argument was live from the start. It struggles to explain why people would then die for something they knew they had faked. It does not struggle as much as apologetics sometimes claims. Let it be a real objection.
- *"Mark's ending was changed, so the whole thing was edited."* Careful. The addition is visible, documented, and footnoted in their own Bible. That is the tradition being caught in the act and the correction being published. It argues for an open record, not a hidden one.
- *"You can't prove a miracle."* Agree. Say so directly.

**Facilitation notes.**
- Do not run the "minimal facts" apologetic as though it is a knockdown. It is a real approach with real scholars behind it, and it is also contested. Name it as one voice.
- The temptation tonight is to push. Resist it. The room is already carrying more than usual, and pressure now will cost you people who would otherwise still be here in three weeks.
- Someone may become emotional. Let them, and do not make them the centre of attention.
- Someone may go quiet and stay quiet. That is fine. Do not draw them out in front of others. Catch them at the door.
- If asked what you think, say it, and label it **my read**.
