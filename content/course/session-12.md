**The question**
What would a daily rhythm with God actually look like, for someone like me?

**The texts, side by side**

| Matthew 6:9–13 | Luke 11:2–4 |
|---|---|
| Our Father in heaven, may your name be honoured. | Father, may your name be honoured. |
| Your kingdom come. Your will be done, on earth as in heaven. | Your kingdom come. |
| Give us today our bread for the day. | Give us each day our bread for the day. |
| Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors. | Forgive us our sins, for we also forgive everyone who owes us. |
| Don't lead us into testing, but rescue us from the evil one. | Don't lead us into testing. |

*(close renderings)*

*Kind of writing: a prayer Jesus taught his followers to pray. Two versions survive, which tells you it was used, not just recorded.*

**What we know**
- Luke's version is shorter. Many scholars think it's closer to the original length, and Matthew's wording closer to how it was prayed in worship. *[debated]*
- Jesus called God "Father" (in Aramaic, *abba*) in a direct, family way. *[strong majority]*
- The prayer is mostly about God's purposes (name, kingdom, will) before our needs (bread, forgiveness, protection). *[consensus]*
- "Debts" is literal and moral at once: in Jesus' world, debt was a crushing daily reality. *[consensus]*
- Practices like *lectio divina* (slow reading) and the examen are centuries old and have been used by ordinary people, not only monks. *[consensus]*

**Try this**
Design a daily rhythm of fifteen minutes that you'll actually keep. Small enough to be boring.
For example: five minutes of silence, five minutes reading a few verses of Mark slowly, the
Lord's Prayer, and the examen at night.

**For the table**
1. What's in both versions of the prayer? What's only in one?
2. What does the order of the requests teach about what prayer is?
3. Which line of the prayer is hardest for you to say honestly?
4. Someone says: "I tried praying and felt nothing." What would you say to them?

**One next step**
Run your rhythm for a week and report what broke.

**Driving question.** Unit D is about living it. People who've said yes, and people still
considering, share the same practical question: what do I actually *do*? This session gives a
simple, sustainable answer, built from the practices they've already tried across the course.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 15 · Practice 25 · Table 25 ·
Invitation 2 · Close 3. *(Longer practice; you'll design rhythms in the room.)*

**The text, how to read it.** Put both versions up side by side. Have one person read
Matthew, another Luke, alternating line by line. Ask people to circle differences. Point out
that Luke's setting is a disciple asking "teach us to pray" (11:1): prayer is learned, not
innate. Everyone starts as a beginner.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *Two versions, one prayer.* Luke's is shorter; Matthew's fuller and more liturgical, with
   lines that fit communal worship. Many scholars think Luke preserves the original length and
   Matthew some original wording. Either way, the early church prayed it, and it shaped their
   lives. *[debated on which is closer; consensus that both reflect use]*

2. *"Father."* Joachim Jeremias argued that Jesus' use of *abba* was unique and intimate
   ("daddy"). That's been overstated: *abba* was also used by adults to their fathers, and
   it's not baby talk. But Jesus' direct, family address to God is characteristic of him, and
   he invited his followers into it. *[strong majority on the directness; the "daddy" claim
   is rejected by most scholars now]*

3. *God's concerns first.* The first half is about God's name, kingdom and will (session 5 in
   one line). Then bread for today, forgiveness, and protection. Prayer starts by being
   re-oriented, not with a shopping list. *[consensus]*

4. *Old practices, not techniques.* *Lectio divina* (Guigo II described its four steps in the
   12th century: read, reflect, pray, rest) is slow, repeated reading of a short passage. The
   examen (session 5) is reviewing the day. Silence (session 3) is the base layer. None of
   them requires feeling anything. *[consensus on the history]*

5. *Feeling nothing is normal.* Many people who pray faithfully go long stretches without
   emotional experience. The Psalms include prayers of silence and absence. A practice is like
   exercise: most days it doesn't feel like much, and over months it changes you. *[my read]*

**The practice, how to introduce it.** Hand out a blank card. "We're going to design your
rhythm now. Fifteen minutes, maximum. Pick from what you've tried: silence, the examen, one
honest sentence, slow reading of Mark, the Lord's Prayer. Write down when and where. The
goal is small enough to be boring. Boring is sustainable." Give ten minutes. Then pair people
up to share.

**Likely objections.**
- *"I don't have fifteen minutes."* Start with five. The rhythm has to fit the actual life.
- *"Written prayers feel fake."* Jesus gave one. Written prayers are training wheels and
  sometimes the only words that come.
- *"What if I'm doing it wrong?"* There's no wrong way to be honest with God.

**Facilitation notes.**
- For people who haven't decided to follow Jesus, frame this as "what it would look like."
  No pressure. They can still try the rhythm.
- Keep it light and practical. This is a workshop night.
- Light invitation: "Next week is the last session. We'll ask everyone, personally, where
