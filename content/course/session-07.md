**The question**
Who was Jesus actually for?

**The text: Mark 2:15–17**
> Jesus was eating at Levi's house, and many tax collectors and "sinners" were at the table with
> him and his followers. When some religious teachers saw it, they asked his disciples, "Why
> does he eat with tax collectors and sinners?" Jesus heard and said, "Healthy people don't
> need a doctor; sick people do. I didn't come to call the righteous, but sinners."
> *(a close rendering)*

*Kind of writing: a short scene that ends on a punchline. Mark builds a whole run of these (2:1–3:6), each one raising the temperature.*

**Also read:** Luke 15:1–2, the complaint that sets up the three "lost" stories.

**What we know**
- Tax collectors in Galilee collected tolls and taxes for Herod Antipas's government, often on contract, and were widely seen as collaborators and cheats. *[strong majority]*
- In Jesus' world, who you ate with said who you were. A shared table meant acceptance. *[consensus]*
- Jesus had a reputation, in his own lifetime, as "a friend of tax collectors and sinners" (Matthew 11:19). It's the kind of insult nobody would invent for their hero. *[consensus that the reputation is historical]*
- The religious teachers who objected weren't cartoon villains. Many were sincere people trying to keep faith alive under pressure. *[consensus]*
- Whether Jesus asked "sinners" to change before or after he ate with them is debated. The stories suggest the welcome came first. *[debated]*

**Try this**
One act of hospitality this week toward someone outside your usual circle. A coffee, a
meal, a real conversation. Then notice how it felt.

**For the table**
1. Who is at Levi's table, and who is standing outside looking in?
2. What did it cost Jesus, socially, to sit there?
3. Who would be "the wrong people" at your table? Who would think *you* were?
4. Someone says: "Welcoming everyone means nothing matters." Does that follow?

**One next step**
Do the act of hospitality. Bring what you noticed next week.

**Driving question.** Session 5 said Jesus announced God's kingdom. This session shows what
it looked like at dinner. The answer to "who was he for?" is scandalously wide, and it
matters for the people in the room, many of whom assume they're the wrong people.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 15 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 2 · Close 3.

**The text, how to read it.** You're eating together as you read this. Say so. Read Mark
2:13–17 aloud, then Luke 15:1–2. Ask the observation questions first: who's at the table,
who's watching, who asks the question, who answers. Point out that the critics ask the
disciples, not Jesus: they're testing the followers. And note the doctor line: Jesus doesn't
deny the guests are sick. He says that's exactly why he's there.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *Who tax collectors were.* In Galilee they worked for Herod Antipas's administration,
   collecting tolls and taxes, often as contractors who kept what they could squeeze above
   the quota. They were seen as collaborators with the occupying order and as dishonest. Levi
   would have been rich and resented. *[strong majority; the details of the tax system are
   debated, so keep it general]*

2. *Meals as statements.* Sharing a table meant accepting someone. Many groups in Jesus'
   time, the Pharisees among them, cared a great deal about purity at meals as a way of
   living out holiness in ordinary life. That was a serious and admirable aim. Jesus'
   practice flipped the logic: holiness wasn't protected by keeping the unclean away; it was
   contagious in the other direction. *[consensus on meals as boundary markers; "contagious
   holiness" is Craig Blomberg's phrase and a fair summary of a strong majority reading]*

3. *The reputation is historical.* "A glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and
   sinners" (Matthew 11:19; Luke 7:34) is an insult. The early church wouldn't invent it.
   That's the criterion of embarrassment from session 3, working again. *[consensus]*

4. *Be fair to the Pharisees.* It's easy, and wrong, to make them the bad guys. They were a
   popular lay movement trying to keep Israel faithful. Jesus argued with them because he was
   close to them, not far away. Christian caricatures of Pharisees have fed centuries of
   contempt for Jews; don't repeat them. Amy-Jill Levine, a Jewish New Testament scholar, is
   excellent on this. *[consensus among scholars; a common popular error]*

5. *Welcome first, then change.* E. P. Sanders argued that what scandalised people wasn't
   that Jesus called sinners to repent (everyone did that) but that he seemed to offer them
   a place in the kingdom without first requiring the usual steps. Others think he did call
   for change and the meals were part of the call. Both agree the welcome was real and
   lives changed at that table: Zacchaeus (Luke 19) is the clearest case. *[debated]*

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "Jesus' most controversial habit was eating with
people. So this week's practice is the same. Not a project, not a charity outreach. One
person, one table, one real conversation." Some will find this easy, some terrifying. Both
are fine.

**Likely objections.**
- *"If everyone's welcome, nothing matters."* Jesus' welcome was not indifference. The doctor
  image assumes something's wrong and needs healing. Welcome is how the healing starts, not
  a statement that there's nothing to heal.
- *"The church today does the opposite."* Often true. Concede it. Then ask: whose example
  are we looking at tonight?
- *"Weren't the Pharisees hypocrites?"* Some people in every movement are. Jesus criticised
  some of them sharply. But the Gospels also have Pharisees who warn him (Luke 13:31) and
  help bury him (Nicodemus, John 19:39). Don't flatten them.

**Facilitation notes.**
- Many people in the room will privately see themselves as "the wrong people." This session
  is quietly addressed to them. Let that land without pointing at anyone.
- Watch for people who were made to feel like outsiders by a church. This can be tender.
- Light invitation: "He's still eating with people like us. The seat's open."
