**The question**
Christians call the Bible God's word. What do they actually mean, and do I have to read it
flat to take it seriously?

**The texts**
- **John 1:1, 14**: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God… And the Word became flesh and lived among us."
- **2 Timothy 3:16–17**: "All scripture is inspired by God (*God-breathed*) and is useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness."
- **Luke 24:27**: "Beginning with Moses and all the prophets, he interpreted to them the things about himself in all the scriptures."

*Kinds of writing: John 1 is a poetic prologue; 2 Timothy is a pastoral letter; Luke 24 is narrative.*

**What Christians actually say**
- The "Word of God," in John 1, is a person: "the Word became flesh." The first and deepest meaning of the phrase is Jesus, not a book. The Bible is the church's trusted witness that points to him. *[the plain reading of John 1; widely held across the church]*
- One way to hold it together: revealed (Jesus), written (Scripture), proclaimed (a sermon): all called "word" because the last two point to the first. *[Barth's model; one major view]*
- On how the Bible was written, two main views: (a) God worked through real people writing as people of their time, with their own idioms and limits, the way God worked through a real human life in Jesus; (b) the text, rightly interpreted, contains no error in anything it affirms. *[two major views; both are held by thoughtful people]*
- "Take it seriously, not literally." Reading each part as the kind of writing it is (poem as poem, parable as parable) is how nearly all biblical scholars read, and it is often the *more* faithful reading. *[near consensus as a method]*

**Try this**
Read Psalm 23 three ways this week: once for information, once slowly as poetry, once out
loud. Notice what each reading gives you, and what it misses.

**For the table**
1. If the "Word of God" is first a person and not a book, what changes about any pressure you feel around the Bible?
2. Genesis 1 as a poem versus Genesis 1 as a science report: what does each reading let you see, and what does each cost?
3. Where have you seen the Bible read badly by being read flat?

**One next step**
When you read this week, ask "what kind of writing is this?" before "what does it tell me
to do?"

<!-- facilitator -->

**Driving question.** A newcomer often assumes "believe the Bible" means "defend every
sentence of a 2,000-year-old book, literally, or you're out." That is a heavier and
stranger burden than what most of the church actually asks. This session hands them a way
to take the Bible with complete seriousness without pretending it is something it is not.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 25 · Practice 10 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 2 · Close 3.

**The text, how to read it.** Read John 1:1–14 aloud; it is rhythmic, meant to be heard.
Point out the move in verse 14: "the Word" (*logos*), abstract and cosmic through verse 13,
suddenly "became flesh." Then 2 Timothy 3:16: note it says *useful*, for a purpose, inside
a specific pastoral letter, and that "scripture" there means the Jewish scriptures. Then
Luke 24:27: the risen Jesus walks two people through "Moses and all the prophets" and
interprets "the things about himself." The pattern in all three: Scripture's job is to
point to Jesus.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *The Word is a person first.* John opens with cosmic language and lands it in a human
   life. Christians who talk about "the Word of God" are, at the deepest level, talking
   about Jesus. The Bible is the church's trusted witness to him. Luther's image: Scripture
   is "the manger" and "the swaddling clothes" that hold the Christ; you go to it for him.
   *[John 1 plain reading; Luther, Prefaces to the Old Testament, 1523]*
2. *A way to hold it together.* Karl Barth described a "threefold form of the Word of God":
   revealed (Jesus, the Word himself), written (Scripture, which testifies to him), and
   proclaimed (preaching, when it faithfully points there). One reality, three forms, Jesus
   at the centre. It keeps the Bible essential without making it a fourth person of the
   Trinity. *[Barth, Church Dogmatics I/1 §4; one major view]*
3. *How it got written, two models.*
   - **Incarnational / accommodation.** God spoke through real people who wrote as people
     of their time, with their own idioms and their own assumptions about the cosmos. The
     humanness is the medium, not a flaw to explain away, like the humanity of Jesus.
     Calvin said God "lisps" to us the way a nurse talks to an infant. *[Enns, Inspiration
     and Incarnation; one major view, common in the mainline and a growing evangelical
     stream]*
   - **Inerrancy.** Scripture, rightly interpreted, teaches no error in anything it
     affirms, including history and science. *[the other major view, dominant in
     conservative evangelicalism; the Chicago Statement, 1978]*
   Present both fairly. Say which is yours as "my read," briefly, then move on.
4. *Serious, not literal.* To take a text seriously is to read it as what it is. Genesis 1
   is a tightly built poem (seven days, forming then filling, a refrain) making a claim
   about who made the world and why, using the cosmology everyone in that world assumed.
   Reading it as a lab report asks it a question it never set out to answer, and you miss
   the actual claim. Same with parables (true, not factual), apocalyptic (coded resistance
   writing, not a timetable), Job (a philosophical drama). "Literal" often collapses into
   "flat," and flat is frequently the least faithful reading. This is not a liberal dodge;
   it is how the text asks to be read, and it is the genre step behind this whole course.
   *[Borg, Reading the Bible Again for the First Time; Walton, The Lost World of Genesis
   One; near consensus as scholarly method]*

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "You already do this. You don't read a text from a
friend the way you read an instruction manual. The Bible has both kinds of writing in it,
and more. This week, catch yourself choosing a reading."

**Likely objections.**
- *"So you just pick which bits to believe."* No. Genre is not a menu; it is a question of
  what the author was doing. You don't get to call the resurrection a metaphor because it
  is inconvenient; the texts present it as an event and the earliest Christians staked
  their lives on it. Reading well cuts both ways.
- *"This sounds like you don't really trust the Bible."* I trust it to do what it is for:
  to hand me Jesus and to form a people. I don't trust it to be a physics textbook,
  because it never claimed to be one.
- *"My church says every word is literally true."* That is a real and serious position,
  held by a lot of thoughtful people. Here is what it is responding to, and here is where
  others land differently. You don't have to resolve that tonight to keep going.
- *"If Genesis 1 isn't history, why believe the resurrection?"* Different kinds of writing,
  doing different jobs, with different evidence and different claims. We give the
  resurrection a whole session (session 9). Hold the question until then.

**Facilitation notes.**
- Hardest session for people from a strict-inerrancy background *and* for people who left
  the church over exactly this. Both are in the room. Do not score points against
  fundamentalism; it reads as contempt and shuts down the person still tender about it.
- Keep "my read" genuinely brief. This session is about giving people room, not recruiting
  them to a camp.
- If someone is clearly relieved ("wait, I'm allowed to read it like this?"), that is the
  session working. Let them say it.
