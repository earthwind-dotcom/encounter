**The question**
What actually got Jesus executed?

**The text: Mark 11:15–18**
> In Jerusalem, Jesus went into the temple and began driving out the people buying and selling
> there. He overturned the money changers' tables... and said, "Isn't it written, 'My house
> will be called a house of prayer for all nations'? But you've made it a hideout for robbers."
> The chief priests and the scribes heard it, and began looking for a way to kill him.
> *(a close rendering)*

**Then read:** the hearing before the high priest (Mark 14:53–65) and before Pilate (15:1–15).

*Kind of writing: the climax of Mark's story. About a third of Mark is the last week of Jesus' life.*

**What we know**
- Jesus was crucified by Roman authority under Pontius Pilate, around the year 30. *[consensus]*
- Crucifixion was Rome's punishment for slaves, rebels and people who threatened public order. It was designed to be public, slow and shameful. *[consensus]*
- The sign on the cross read "The King of the Jews." That's a political charge. *[strong majority that the charge is historical]*
- Some kind of disturbance at the temple, shortly before his death, was very probably the trigger. *[strong majority]*
- Temple leaders and Roman authority both played a part. How much each, and what the Jewish hearing was like, is debated. *[debated]*
- "The Jews killed Jesus" as a blanket charge is wrong, and has been used to justify terrible violence against Jewish people. *[rejected]*

**Try this**
Honest prayer. Once a day, say one true sentence out loud to God. Even if it's anger. Even if
it's "I don't know if you're there."

**For the table**
1. What exactly does Jesus do and say in the temple? What does he quote?
2. Why would a religious act at the temple look like a political threat to Rome?
3. Mark spends a third of his book on one week. What does that tell you about what he thinks matters?
4. Someone says: "He was just in the wrong place at the wrong time." Does the evidence support that?

**One next step**
One honest sentence to God, every day this week.

**Driving question.** People know Jesus died on a cross. Few know why he was killed, as a
matter of history. This session answers that, and it sets up the next unit: before we ask what
his death *meant* (session 10), we need to know what it *was*.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 15 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 2 · Close 3.

**The text, how to read it.** Read Mark 11:15–18 aloud. Point out the two quotations Jesus
uses: Isaiah 56:7 ("a house of prayer for all nations") and Jeremiah 7:11 ("a den of
robbers"). Jeremiah 7 is a prophecy of the temple's destruction. Quoting it in the temple
courts, at Passover, with crowds and Roman soldiers nearby, was explosive. Then read the two
hearings. Ask people to track the charges: what is Jesus accused of before the high priest?
Before Pilate?

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *Crucifixion was a message.* Rome used it for slaves and rebels, along roads, in public,
   to deter. It was slow and humiliating. Martin Hengel's short book documents this well.
   Nobody in the ancient world thought crucifixion was a noble death. *[consensus]*

2. *The charge was political.* "King of the Jews" on the sign (Mark 15:26) is the kind of
   charge Rome executed people for: a rival claim to rule. The early Christians had every
   reason to play this down, and they kept it. *[strong majority]*

3. *The temple was the trigger.* E. P. Sanders argued that Jesus' action in the temple, a
   symbolic act pointing to its coming destruction and renewal, is the most likely immediate
   cause of his arrest. Passover packed Jerusalem with pilgrims; the authorities feared riots.
   One man causing a disturbance in the temple courts and speaking of its fall was a public
   order problem. *[strong majority that the temple incident was the trigger; what exactly
   Jesus meant by it is debated]*

4. *Who was responsible.* The Gospels show a collaboration: temple leaders who saw Jesus as a
   danger, and a Roman governor who had the power to execute. How formal the Jewish hearing
   was, and how much the Gospels shift blame from Pilate to the priests, is debated; Raymond
   Brown's two-volume study is the standard treatment. *[debated]* What's not debated: only
   Rome crucified, and the idea that "the Jews" as a people killed Jesus is false and has done
   enormous harm. The Catholic Church formally rejected it at Vatican II (*Nostra Aetate*,
   1965). *[rejected]*

5. *Each Gospel tells it with its own emphasis.* Mark stresses abandonment ("My God, why have
   you forsaken me?"). Luke stresses innocence and forgiveness ("Father, forgive them"). John
   stresses control and completion ("It is finished"). Same event, different lenses. That
   matters for session 10. *[consensus]*

6. *He saw it coming.* Most scholars think Jesus went to Jerusalem knowing the danger, and
   that he understood his coming death as connected to his mission, though exactly how he
   interpreted it is debated. *[strong majority that he anticipated a violent end]*

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "This week we move from silence to words. One true
sentence, out loud, to God. Not polished. Not religious-sounding. The Psalms are full of
people doing exactly this, and Jesus did it on the cross, quoting one." Make clear that
anger and doubt count.

**Likely objections.**
- *"The Jews killed Jesus."* Correct this clearly and kindly. Jesus and all his first
  followers were Jews. Rome executed him. Some temple leaders were involved; a crowd was
  involved. None of that makes a people guilty, then or now.
- *"Maybe he was a failed rebel."* He refused armed revolt (Mark 14:48; session 6), and Rome
  didn't round up his followers, which it did with actual rebels. That suggests Pilate saw
  him as a nuisance, not a general.
- *"Did he want to die?"* He prayed not to (Mark 14:36). He went anyway. That's different.
