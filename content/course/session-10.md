**The question**
Why do Christians say a first-century execution matters for me?

**The text: Mark 10:45**
> "The Son of Man didn't come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for
> many." *(a close rendering)*

**Also read:** Isaiah 52:13–53:12 (the servant who suffers), and Romans 3:21–26.

*Kind of writing: Mark 10:45 is a saying of Jesus. Isaiah 53 is a poem, written centuries earlier. Romans 3 is a letter, dense and argued. Each says something different about the same death.*

**What we know**
- The New Testament uses several pictures for what Jesus' death did: a ransom paid, a sacrifice offered, a victory won over evil, an example of love, a debt cancelled, enemies reconciled. *[consensus]*
- No single "theory" of the cross is the New Testament's own. The formal theories came centuries later. *[consensus]*
- The first Christians read Isaiah 53 in the light of Jesus' death very early. Whether Jesus himself drew on it is debated. *[strong majority on the early church; debated on Jesus]*
- The images borrow from the temple, the marketplace, the law court and the battlefield: the places where people of that world knew what it meant to be freed, forgiven or put right. *[consensus]*
- *My read:* all the images point to one offer. What you carry and can't put down, he'll take.

**Try this**
Name one thing you carry that you can't seem to put down. Write it on a piece of paper. Keep
the paper.

**For the table**
1. In Mark 10:45, who serves whom? What is the "ransom" for?
2. Which of the pictures (ransom, sacrifice, victory, reconciliation, forgiveness of debt) makes most sense to you? Which makes least?
3. Why would the New Testament need more than one picture?
4. Someone says: "A God who needs blood to forgive isn't a God I could love." What would you say?

**One next step**
Hold on to the paper. Session 11 comes back to it.

<!-- facilitator -->

**Driving question.** Last session established that Jesus was executed, and session 9 that his
followers said he rose. Now the question every outsider eventually asks: so what? Why is his
death supposed to matter for me? This is where many people have heard only one version (often
a harsh one: an angry God who needs punishing someone) and rejected it. The honest answer is
richer, and more personal.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 15 · Table 25 ·
Invitation 7 · Close 3. *(Invitation longer this week.)*

**The text, how to read it.** Read Mark 10:45 in its context first (10:35–45: two disciples
ask for the best seats; Jesus answers with service and death). Then read Isaiah 53 aloud
slowly, with a different reader for each stanza. Ask: what does the servant carry, and for
whom? Then read Romans 3:21–26 once, and don't explain it in detail yet: just notice the
words "gift," "redemption" and "put right."

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *Many pictures, one event.* The New Testament doesn't give one explanation. It gives a
   gallery: ransom (Mark 10:45), sacrifice (Hebrews 9), victory over the powers (Colossians
   2:15), reconciliation of enemies (2 Corinthians 5:18–21), a debt cancelled (Colossians
   2:14), an example to follow (1 Peter 2:21). Each picture shows something the others miss.
   *[consensus]*

2. *The theories came later.* Christians over the centuries built these pictures into formal
   theories: "Christus Victor" (the cross defeats evil), satisfaction (Anselm, 11th century),
   penal substitution (strongly developed in the Reformation), moral influence (Abelard).
   Different traditions weight them differently. None is the New Testament's only word.
   *[consensus on the history; debated which should be central]*

3. *The images come from real life.* Ransom was what you paid to free a slave or a prisoner.
   Sacrifice was how people in that world knew that wrongs could be dealt with and
   relationships restored. The law court knew what it was to be declared in the right. The
   point isn't the mechanics; it's that each world knew what freedom looked like, and said:
   this is that. *[consensus]*

4. *Isaiah 53.* A servant who carries others' pain and wrongs, is crushed, and is vindicated.
   The first Christians saw Jesus there very early (Acts 8:32–35). The original poem's
   reference (Israel? a prophet?) is debated among scholars; Jewish readers have their own
   long tradition of reading it. Say that honestly. *[debated on the original reference;
   consensus that early Christians applied it to Jesus]*

5. *God isn't angry and Jesus kind.* A common and damaging picture is an angry Father
   punishing a gentle Son. The New Testament says the opposite: "God was in Christ,
   reconciling the world to himself" (2 Corinthians 5:19). The one who suffers is God himself,
   come in person, taking the damage rather than handing it out. *[my read, but it's the
   mainstream of the tradition]*

6. *The offer underneath.* Every image ends in the same place: something done for us that we
   couldn't do, freely given. The old word is grace. *[my read]*

**The invitation (explicit).** Before the practice, say something like this, in your own
words:

> "Everything we've looked at points to one offer. Whatever you carry, the wrongs you've done,
> the ones done to you, the weight you can't put down, Jesus' death says he'll take it. Not
> because you've earned it. As a gift.
>
> If you want to hand it over tonight, you can. You don't need special words. You can tell him,
> silently or out loud, that you're done carrying it alone and you want to trust him. If you're
> not there, that's a real answer. Keep coming. We mean that."

Then move to the practice. No hands raised, no spotlight. Be available after.

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "Write down one thing you carry. Nobody will see it.
Fold it. Keep it in your pocket or your wallet this week. Notice when you feel its weight."

**Likely objections.**
- *"Why does God need a sacrifice at all? Why not just forgive?"* A real question, and
  theologians have argued about it for a thousand years. One honest answer: forgiveness
  always costs the forgiver something; someone absorbs the loss. The cross says God absorbed
  it himself. Label that as one way of seeing it.
- *"Penal substitution is cosmic child abuse."* A phrase some writers have used. Take the
  concern seriously: if it's told as an angry Father and a victim Son, it's been told badly.
  The Son is God, choosing to give himself. Point to the range of images.
- *"This is just guilt-tripping."* The offer is freedom from guilt, not more of it. If anyone
  leaves feeling worse, the facilitator has done it wrong.

**Facilitation notes.**
- People who grew up with a fearful or harsh version of the cross may react strongly. Let them.
  Don't defend a version you wouldn't recognise either.
