**The question**
Love your enemies. Turn the other cheek. Is that a literal instruction, or a nice ideal nobody
is expected to live?

**The text: Matthew 5:38–41, 44**
> You have heard that it was said, "An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth." But I tell you,
> don't resist an evildoer. If anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn the other one to him
> as well. If anyone takes you to court for your shirt, let him have your coat too. If someone
> forces you to go one mile, go with him two... Love your enemies, and pray for those who
> persecute you. *(a close rendering)*

*Kind of writing: vivid teaching scenes. Short, concrete, deliberately startling cases meant to train a way of being, not a legal code.*

**What we know**
- "Eye for an eye" in the Old Testament was a *limit* on revenge: no more than the harm done. *[consensus]*
- A slap on the *right* cheek, from a right-handed person, is a backhand: an insult from a superior, meant to humiliate, not injure. *[strong majority]*
- The "shirt and coat" are inner and outer garments. The outer cloak was legally protected; you couldn't keep a poor person's cloak overnight (Exodus 22:26–27). *[consensus]*
- "Forces you to go a mile" uses a technical word for a soldier requisitioning labour. Simon of Cyrene is "forced" with the same word to carry Jesus' cross (Matthew 27:32). *[consensus]*
- Walter Wink read these as creative, nonviolent ways of refusing humiliation: neither hitting back nor rolling over. Many scholars accept the setting and debate how tactical Jesus meant it to be. *[debated]*

**Try this**
Name one relationship where the urge to hit back is live right now. Don't act. Just sit with
it for five minutes and notice what the urge wants.

**For the table**
1. In each of the three scenes, who has the power, and who ends up holding the initiative?
2. What would these scenes have meant to people living under Roman soldiers and debt courts?
3. Where in your life would "neither hit back nor roll over" look different from what you usually do?
4. Someone says: "This is a recipe for being walked on, and it's dangerous advice for abuse victims." How do you respond?

**One next step**
For one week, don't send the sentence that would win and also wound.

<!-- facilitator -->

**Driving question.** Everyone knows "turn the other cheek." Almost everyone hears it as
"be a doormat." Some have had it used against them. This session shows that the text is
sharper and stranger than that, and then it doesn't let anyone off the hook: Jesus really did
mean enemy-love, and it's hard.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 15 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 2 · Close 3.

**The text, how to read it.** Read 5:38–48 aloud. Then set up the structure on a whiteboard:
the old measure (5:38), the new posture (5:39a), four scenes (cheek, garment, mile, the one
who asks), then the principle (5:43–48, love your enemies, "so that you may be children of
your Father," who sends rain on the just and unjust). The scenes are worked examples of the
principle, not the principle itself. Have people act out the right-cheek slap (gently, with a
right hand): it can only be a backhand. The room will get it immediately.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *The old law was already a brake.* "Eye for eye" (Exodus 21:24; Leviticus 24:20;
   Deuteronomy 19:21) limited vengeance to proportion. Jesus goes further: remove the
   return entirely. *[consensus]*

2. *The scenes are about humiliation, not injury.* A backhand on the right cheek was how a
   superior insulted an inferior. Turning the left cheek says, without a word: hit me as an
   equal, if you're going to. Being sued for your inner garment means you're a debtor with
   nothing else; handing over the outer one too leaves you naked in court, which in that culture
   shamed the onlooker more than the naked person, and exposes what the debt system is doing to
   the poor. Soldiers could compel a civilian to carry gear for a limited distance; going a
   second mile takes the initiative back and puts the soldier in an awkward spot. *[the social
   setting is strong majority; the specific one-mile legal limit is less certain, so don't
   overstate it]*

3. *Wink's "third way".* Walter Wink made this reading famous: neither fight nor flight but
   creative, nonviolent resistance that keeps your dignity and exposes the injustice. It has
   been hugely influential. *[debated]* The pushback: some scholars (Luz, for example) think
   Wink makes the scenes more tactical than Matthew intended, and that "do not resist the
   evildoer" is at heart a renunciation of retaliation and personal vindication. Both readings
   agree on the core: no revenge, no mirroring the violence. They differ on how clever the
   illustrations are meant to be.

4. *The word "resist".* The Greek *antistēnai* often means to stand against someone in armed
   opposition (it's common for military resistance in the Greek Old Testament). That supports
   reading 5:39 as "don't meet violence with violence," not "do nothing." *[my read, with good
   support]*

5. *Enemy-love is the point, and it's grounded in God.* The reason given is the character of
   God, who is generous to the good and the evil (5:45). This isn't strategy; it's family
   resemblance. And Jesus lived it: he didn't retaliate at his arrest and prayed for his
   executioners (Luke 23:34). *[consensus on the text]*

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "We're not going to do anything with the anger
tonight. We're just going to look at it. Pick one person. Five minutes. Notice what the urge
to hit back wants: to be seen, to be right, to be safe? Don't judge it." Warn people this
can stir real pain. Have a quiet exit available.

**Likely objections.**
- *"This is dangerous advice for someone being abused."* Take this seriously and say so
  plainly: Jesus is not telling anyone to stay in danger. The scenes are about refusing
  humiliation and keeping your dignity, not submitting to harm. Leaving, getting help, and
  going to the police are all consistent with this teaching. If this comes up personally,
  follow up one to one and point to real help.
- *"Nobody can actually live this."* Some people have, at great cost: Martin Luther King Jr.
  drew directly on this teaching. And the Sermon ends with "be perfect (whole) as your Father
  is perfect," which is a direction, not a test you pass on day one.
- *"So Christians can't defend anyone?"* Christians have disagreed on this for centuries
  (pacifist traditions versus just-war traditions). It's debated; name both.
- *"Isn't this passive?"* Show them again: every response is active. That's the surprise.

**Facilitation notes.**
- This is a heavy session for anyone who was told to "turn the other cheek" to an abuser.
  Watch faces. Don't put anyone on the spot.
- Resist turning it into a tips-for-conflict night. The hardest line is 5:44, and the reason
  is 5:45. Land there.
- Light invitation: "Following Jesus includes this. It's not easy, and you don't do it alone."
