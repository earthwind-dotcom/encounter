**The question**
What did his first hearers assume that we have to be told?

**The text: Luke 15:11–12, 20**
> Then Jesus said, "There was a man who had two sons. The younger of them said to his
> father, 'Father, give me the share of the property that will belong to me.' So he divided
> his property between them." … "So he set off and went to his father. But while he was
> still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and put his arms
> around him and kissed him." *(NRSV)*

*Kind of writing: a story told out loud, to a mixed crowd, with a target. Luke 15:1–2 tells you who was standing there and what they were complaining about.*

**What we know**
- Jesus was a first-century Galilean Jew speaking to first-century Jews under Roman occupation. Everything he says is aimed at people who already knew things we have to be told. *[consensus]*
- Family property, honor and public reputation were not private matters. They belonged to the whole village. A son asking for his share early was doing something socially violent, whatever its exact legal status. *[consensus on the social world; the precise legal mechanics are less certain]*
- An older man of standing running in public would have had to gather up his robe to do it. In that culture it cost him dignity in front of everyone watching. *[one major view, associated with Kenneth Bailey]*
- The Pharisees were a serious renewal movement trying to help ordinary people live faithfully, not villains in a costume. Most of what the Gospels show Jesus arguing with them about is the kind of argument insiders have. *[consensus]*

**Try this**
Read one page of any of the four accounts of Jesus' life. Just one page. Write down every
question it raises for you, including the ones that feel stupid or hostile. Do not try to
answer any of them.

**For the table**
1. What did you assume about this story before tonight, and where did that assumption come from?
2. If the father losing dignity in public is the point, what is the story actually claiming about God?
3. The story stops without telling you what the older brother decided. Why do you think Jesus left it there?

**One next step**
Bring your three best questions to session 5.

<!-- facilitator -->

**Driving question.** People read the Bible as though it were written to them, in their
century, with their assumptions. It was not. This session is about learning to ask what the
first hearers already knew, which is the single most useful reading habit the course can
hand anyone.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 15 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 2 · Close 3.

**The text, how to read it.** Read Luke 15:1–2 first, before the parable. It sets the
scene: tax collectors and others with bad reputations are coming to listen, and the
religious people are grumbling that Jesus eats with them. The story is an answer to that
complaint. It is not a general-purpose story about family. It is aimed.

Then read 15:11–32 straight through. Then go back and ask the group to notice four things:
the younger son asks for property while his father is alive; the father agrees; the father
sees him "while he was still far off," which means he was watching the road; and the story
ends with the older brother outside and no resolution.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *Everything here is public.* In a village, family business was community business.
   Shame and honor were not feelings, they were currency. The younger son's request insults
   the father in front of everyone, and the father's response, twice, is to absorb a public
   cost rather than defend his standing. *[consensus on honor and shame as the operating
   social system]*

2. *The running.* An older man of position did not run. Doing it meant hiking up his robe
   and exposing his legs, which was undignified in front of the whole village. Kenneth
   Bailey argued that the father runs to reach his son before the village does, taking the
   humiliation onto himself. It is a beautiful reading, and it is one scholar's
   reconstruction rather than a settled fact. Say which it is. *[one major view]*

3. *A caution worth giving out loud.* Bailey built much of his reading on modern Middle
   Eastern village life, and other scholars have questioned whether that is a safe window
   onto the first century. Amy-Jill Levine has pressed a sharper objection: that the
   standard Christian reading of this parable works by making Judaism look harsh so that
   Jesus can look generous. She points out that a father forgiving a son is not a scandal
   in Jewish tradition, it is Jewish tradition. Take that seriously in the room. *[debated,
   and the objection is a fair one]*

4. *The Pharisees.* Almost everyone in the group arrives with a cartoon. The Pharisees were
   a reform movement trying to make holiness livable for ordinary people outside the
   temple system. Jesus argues with them the way people argue inside a family, sharply and
   at close range. E. P. Sanders spent a career dismantling the idea that Judaism was a
   religion of joyless rule-keeping that Jesus came to fix. *[consensus among specialists,
   and still not widely known in churches]*

5. *What the habit is.* Before asking what a passage means for you, ask what it meant to
   the people standing there. That order matters, and it is the reading posture session 2
   set up.

**The practice, how to introduce it.** "Your job this week is not to understand anything.
It is to notice. One page, every question it raises, written down. The questions you're
embarrassed by are the valuable ones, so write those down first." This is the observation
step, and it is what every serious reader does before interpreting.

**Likely objections.**
- *"So I need a history degree to read this?"* No. You need to know that you have assumptions, and to hold them loosely. Most of what you need is in the footnotes of a decent study Bible.
- *"If the meaning was hidden in ancient culture, how is this a book for everyone?"* The main thing in this story is not hidden at all: a father wants his kid back. The cultural detail sharpens it. It does not unlock it.
- *"Weren't the Pharisees the bad guys, though?"* Some of Jesus' hardest words are aimed at them, yes. That is different from them being a cartoon. And note who invited him to dinner repeatedly in Luke.
- *"This feels like you're explaining away the offense."* Fair. The offense is real. The question is whether it lands where we think it lands.

**Facilitation notes.**
- Somebody in the room has been told the prodigal story their whole life and is bored. The Levine objection will wake them up. Use it.
- Somebody else in the room is the older brother and knows it. Do not chase that. Just leave the ending open, the way the story does.
- Resist tidying up the parable's silence about the older brother. Jesus left it unresolved for the people who were listening, who were the older brothers.
- The observation practice pays off for the rest of the course. Sell it properly.
