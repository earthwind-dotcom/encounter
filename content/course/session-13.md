**The question**
Is following Jesus a solo thing, or not? And if not, why would I trust a church?

**The text: Acts 2:42, 44–47**
> They devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching, to sharing life together, to breaking
> bread and to prayer... All the believers were together and had everything in common. They
> sold property and possessions to give to anyone in need... They ate together in their homes
> with glad and generous hearts. *(a close rendering)*

**Also read:** 1 Corinthians 12:12–27 ("you are the body of Christ").

*Kind of writing: Acts 2 is a summary portrait of the earliest community, idealised on purpose. 1 Corinthians is a letter to a real, messy church that was getting a lot wrong.*

**What we know**
- "Church" (*ekklēsia*) meant a gathering of people, not a building or an institution. *[consensus]*
- The earliest Christians met in homes, ate together, and were known for caring for the poor, the sick and the abandoned. *[consensus]*
- The church has also done great harm: abuse, violence, racism, and cover-ups. That's documented history. *[consensus]*
- Paul's letters were mostly written to churches that were failing in some way. The mess isn't new. *[consensus]*
- *My read:* you can't follow Jesus alone for long. Not because the church is impressive, but because he made his people a body.

**Try this**
Come to one gathering of the wider church, as an observer, with no expectations. Notice what
you notice.

**For the table**
1. What did the first community actually do together, according to Acts 2?
2. In 1 Corinthians 12, what does Paul say about the parts of the body that seem weaker?
3. What would a church have to be like for you to trust it?
4. Someone says: "I can follow Jesus on my own; I don't need organised religion." What's true in that, and what's missing?

**One next step**
In the coming week, a facilitator will ask you personally: "Where are you with following
Jesus?" Every honest answer is fine.

<!-- facilitator -->

**Driving question.** This is the last session, and it carries two weights. The honest one:
the church's record is mixed, and some people in the room have been hurt by it. The personal
one: this is where each person is asked directly where they are. The session has to hold
both: no defensiveness about the church, and no pressure in the question.

**Run of show.** Meal 20 · Question 5 · Text 15 · Brief 20 · Practice 5 · Table 30 ·
Invitation 10 · Close 5. *(A longer close: this is the end of the course.)*

**The text, how to read it.** Read Acts 2:42–47 aloud. Ask what they did: teaching, shared
life, meals, prayer, generosity. Then read 1 Corinthians 12:12–27. Point out verse 22: "the
parts of the body that seem weaker are indispensable." And point out that Paul is writing to
a church with divisions, lawsuits and people humiliating the poor at the Lord's Supper
(1 Corinthians 11:17–22). Acts shows the aim; Corinthians shows the reality. Both are in the
Bible.

**The brief, say roughly this.**

1. *Church means people.* *Ekklēsia* was an ordinary Greek word for an assembly of citizens.
   The first Christians used it for their gatherings in homes. No buildings for about two
   centuries. *[consensus]*

2. *What they were known for.* The sociologist Rodney Stark argued that Christian care for the
   sick during the epidemics of the 2nd and 3rd centuries, when others fled, was one reason
   the movement grew. Larry Hurtado describes how strange the early churches were to Romans:
   mixed classes, mixed ethnicities, no statues, care for the unwanted. *[strong majority on
   the care; Stark's growth figures are debated]*

3. *The record, honestly.* The church has also blessed wars, covered up abuse, and defended
   slavery and racism, sometimes with the Bible in hand. Christians also led movements against
   the same things. Tom Holland's *Dominion* traces how deeply Christian ideas (like the dignity
   of the weak) shaped the West, including its critiques of the church. Say all of this
   without defending the indefensible. *[consensus]*

4. *Why not solo?* The New Testament never imagines a follower of Jesus without others. Paul's
   picture is a body: you can't be a hand on your own. Practically, the things that keep people
   going (being known, being challenged, being cared for in a crisis, serving others) need
   people. The vault's own principle applies: a decision with no community leads nowhere.
   *[my read, with the whole New Testament behind it]*

5. *What a healthy church looks like.* It tells the truth, including about itself. It welcomes
   questions. Its leaders are accountable. It lets you say no. Give people permission to walk
   away from a church that can't do these things. *[my read]*

**The practice, how to introduce it.** Brief tonight. "This week, come to one gathering of
the wider church. Sit at the back. You don't have to sing, give or stand. Just watch."

**The invitation (explicit, and direct).** Take your time. Say something like:

> "We've spent thirteen weeks on this. The sources, the person, the claim, the life. The whole
> course has been heading to one question, and we want to ask it plainly: where are you with
> following Jesus?
>
> There are three honest answers, and all three are fine. 'Yes, and I want to start.' 'Not
> yet.' 'No.' Over the next week, one of us will ask each of you that question, one to one, and
> we'll listen. Whatever you say, there's a next step: Stage 2, practicing, is for anyone who
> wants to keep going. Stage 3 is for anyone who says yes and wants to talk about baptism and
> belonging. And if your answer is no, you've still taken a real course in how the New
> Testament works, and you're welcome back any time."

Then close with thanks, and let people stay and talk.

**Likely objections.**
- *"The church hurt me."* Believe them. Don't defend it. Point to the Hard Question on the site
  and to the healthy-church signs. Offer a one-to-one.
- *"I'm spiritual, not religious."* Honour what's true: Jesus wasn't impressed by religion
  either. Then ask what "not alone" might look like for them.
- *"Which church?"* Be ready with honest, specific options, starting with your own, and don't
  pretend yours is the only good one.

**Facilitation notes.**
- The one-to-ones are the most important part of the whole course. Schedule them before the
  night ends. Ask, then listen. Don't argue. Don't count.
- For a "yes": pray with them if they want, connect them to Stage 3, and make sure they have a
  person, not just a programme.
- For a "not yet" or "no": thank them, mean it, and invite them to Stage 2 or the open Q&A night.
- Never share anyone's answer with anyone else without their permission.
